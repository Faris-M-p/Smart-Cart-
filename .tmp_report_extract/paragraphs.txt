69|FrontTitle|ABSTRACT
70|Normal|Small and medium supermarkets increasingly need an online channel through which customers can browse products and place orders, while the store itself still has to manage a large catalogue, frequent stock intake from suppliers and the processing of customer orders. Maintaining these activities through walk-in sales, manual registers and disconnected spreadsheets makes stock information unreliable and limits the store’s reach.
71|Normal|SmartCart is a web-based e-commerce and management system designed for a single supermarket store (referred to in this report as SmartCart Now). It provides a customer-facing storefront and an employee administration panel within one ASP.NET Core 8 MVC application backed by a relational database. On the storefront, registered customers can browse the catalogue by category, brand and price, view product details and pack-size variants (SKUs), maintain a wishlist and a shopping cart, check out with a saved delivery address using Cash on Delivery, view their order history and cancel orders that are still in an early status.
72|Normal|The administration panel allows store employees to manage categories, sub-categories, brands, products, SKUs, variant attributes and product media; to maintain suppliers and record purchases that create batch-wise stock; to adjust inventory; to process customer orders through confirmation, shipping, delivery or cancellation; to manage homepage banners and featured items; and to manage employees, roles and permissions. Online visibility is controlled per product and per SKU.
73|Normal|Customer operations and admin order processing use stored procedures executed through Dapper, where business rules such as stock availability, online-sale eligibility and order-status transitions are enforced in the database. Other administrative modules use Entity Framework Core without migrations; the schema is created and updated through versioned build and patch scripts. Customers and employees are authenticated through separate JWT cookies, passwords are hashed, and admin actions are protected by role-based, permission-level access control.
74|Normal|Online payment, multi-store and multi-zone operation, advanced analytics, recommendations and a mobile application are not part of the current implementation; they are presented as future scope (SmartCart Then), for which the current architecture provides a foundation.
75|Normal|Keywords: E-Commerce, Supermarket, ASP.NET Core MVC, SQL Server, Stored Procedures, Dapper, Inventory Management, Role-Based Access Control, JWT.

426|Heading1|CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION AND PROJECT OVERVIEW
427|Heading2|1.1 Background
428|Normal|Supermarkets sell a wide range of everyday goods — groceries, packaged food, household items and personal care products — usually in several pack sizes and from many brands. Traditionally, customers visit the store, select items physically and pay at a billing counter. Behind the counter, the store purchases goods from suppliers, records stock, monitors shelf availability and reorders items that are running low.
429|Normal|Many small and medium stores still perform these activities with manual registers, standalone billing software or spreadsheets. Such methods are adequate for walk-in trade but provide no channel for customers who prefer to order from home, and they make it difficult to keep an accurate, up-to-date picture of stock across hundreds of items.
430|Heading2|1.2 E-Commerce Context
431|Normal|Business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce allows a retailer to publish its catalogue on the web and accept orders online. For a supermarket, an e-commerce system must go beyond a simple product list: products exist in multiple variants (for example 500 g and 1 kg packs), each variant has its own price and stock, items must be hidden when they are not meant to be sold online, and orders must be fulfilled from actual stock. The online storefront and the store’s back-office therefore have to share the same catalogue and inventory data.
432|Heading2|1.3 Motivation for SmartCart
433|Normal|The motivation for SmartCart is to provide a single, integrated system in which the store’s administrative data — catalogue, suppliers, purchases and stock — directly drives what customers see and can order online. Instead of maintaining the online catalogue separately from inventory, SmartCart uses one database so that a purchase entry increases available stock, an order reduces it, and a cancelled order restores it.
434|Normal|
435|Heading2|1.4 Introduction to SmartCart
436|Normal|SmartCart is a web-based e-commerce management system implemented as a single ASP.NET Core 8 MVC application with two areas: a public storefront for customers and an admin panel for store employees. Both areas share one relational database.
437|Heading2|1.5 SmartCart Now
438|Normal|Current Architecture / SmartCart Now is a single-store supermarket system. One store operates the platform, one catalogue is maintained, stock is held for that store only and customers place Cash on Delivery orders. There is no multi-store, zone or tenant model in the running application.

440|Normal|Future Architecture / SmartCart Then describes the direction in which the system may be extended: multiple stores or branches, zone-wise administration, per-location inventory, online payment and additional integrations. 480|Heading2|1.7 Problem Statement
481|Normal|A supermarket that relies on walk-in trade and manual or disconnected record-keeping faces the following problems:
482|ListParagraph|Customers cannot view the product range, prices or availability without visiting the store.
483|ListParagraph|There is no channel for customers to place orders remotely and track their status.
484|ListParagraph|Stock is recorded manually or in separate files, so the quantity available for sale is often inaccurate.
485|ListParagraph|Purchases from suppliers are not linked to the stock available for sale.
486|ListParagraph|Products with several pack sizes and prices are difficult to manage consistently.
487|ListParagraph|Access to administrative functions is not controlled per employee or per task.
488|ListParagraph|Order processing (confirmation, dispatch, delivery, cancellation) is not recorded in a structured way.
489|Normal|The problem addressed by this project is therefore: to design and develop a web-based system that lets a single supermarket publish its catalogue online, accept and process customer orders, and manage catalogue, purchases and stock from a secure, role-controlled administration panel using one consistent database.
490|Heading2|1.8 Need for the Proposed System
491|Normal|The need for SmartCart arises from the gap between the store’s internal operations and its customers’ expectations. An integrated system is needed so that (i) the online catalogue reflects the actual catalogue and stock, (ii) orders are validated against available stock before they are accepted, (iii) employees work through defined permissions, and (iv) every order passes through a clear set of statuses. A database-centred design with business rules enforced close to the data was required to keep stock and orders consistent.

577|Heading2|2.1 Existing Shopping System
578|Normal|In the existing arrangement considered for this project, customers buy from the supermarket in person. They select products from the shelves and pay at the counter. Information about products, offers and availability is available only inside the store or by telephone. Orders placed by telephone, where accepted, are written down manually.
579|Heading2|2.2 Existing Manual / Traditional Processes
580|ListParagraph|Purchasing: goods received from suppliers are recorded against supplier invoices in registers or spreadsheets.
581|ListParagraph|Stock: quantities are counted periodically; the relationship between purchases, sales and current stock is not maintained automatically.
582|ListParagraph|Catalogue: product names, pack sizes and prices are maintained informally or only in billing software.
583|ListParagraph|Orders: remote orders, if any, are recorded manually and their status is communicated by phone.
584|ListParagraph|Access: any staff member with access to the records can change them; there is no per-task authorisation.
585|Heading2|2.3 Limitations
586|ListParagraph|No online presence or self-service ordering for customers.
587|ListParagraph|Inaccurate and delayed stock information.
588|ListParagraph|Duplicate data entry between purchase records, stock records and price lists.
589|ListParagraph|No structured order lifecycle or history.
590|ListParagraph|No audit of who changed what and no role-based restrictions.
591|ListParagraph|Difficult to scale as the product range grows.

617|Heading2|2.5 Challenges Faced by Small Retail Businesses
618|Normal|Small retailers typically have limited budgets and technical staff. They need a system that is simple for employees to use, runs on commonly available infrastructure, and does not require separate products for the online shop and the back-office. They also need flexibility: not every item is suitable for online sale, and prices and pack sizes change frequently. At the same time, they must protect customer data and prevent unauthorised changes to prices and stock.
619|Normal|SmartCart addresses these challenges by combining storefront and administration in one application, by providing per-product and per-SKU online visibility, and by enforcing permissions for every administrative action.

621|Heading2|2.6 Proposed System
622|Normal|The proposed system, SmartCart, is a web-based application that serves both customers and employees of a single supermarket. The storefront lets customers find products, choose a SKU, add it to the cart and place an order. The admin panel lets employees maintain all master data and process orders. Business rules that protect stock and order integrity are implemented in stored procedures so that they apply regardless of which screen triggers them.

981|Heading1|CHAPTER 3: LITERATURE REVIEW AND FEASIBILITY
982|Heading2|3.1 Overview
983|Normal|This chapter reviews the concepts and technologies relevant to SmartCart: the structure of e-commerce systems, common architectural patterns, the technologies used to build the system and the security concepts applied. It then assesses the feasibility of the project and identifies the gap that SmartCart fills.
984|Heading2|3.2 E-Commerce Systems
985|Normal|An e-commerce system allows a seller to present products, accept orders and manage fulfilment through the internet. A typical B2C system consists of: a catalogue (products, categories, prices, images), a customer account module, a cart and checkout process, an order management module for the seller, inventory control and payment handling. For grocery and supermarket retail, additional characteristics are important:
986|ListParagraph|Variants and units: the same product is sold in several pack sizes or weights, each with its own price and stock.
987|ListParagraph|Stock accuracy: high item counts and frequent replenishment make real-time stock important.
988|ListParagraph|Selective online sale: some items may be sold in-store only.
989|ListParagraph|Local fulfilment: delivery from a nearby store, often with cash payment on delivery.
990|Normal|SmartCart models these characteristics directly: products have SKUs with units and prices, stock is maintained per SKU, and the SellOnline flag controls online availability at both product and SKU level.
991|Normal|
992|Heading2|3.3 Existing E-Commerce Platforms
993|Normal|Existing approaches available to a supermarket can be grouped into three categories. (1) Hosted marketplace or delivery platforms, where the store lists its products on a third-party platform; these provide reach but limited control over data, catalogue structure and fees. (2) General-purpose e-commerce software, which provides online shop features but often requires separate back-office or inventory tools and customisation for pack-size variants and purchases. (3) Stand-alone billing or inventory software, which supports in-store operations but has no customer-facing channel.
994|Normal|This review is limited to these general categories; no claims are made about the features, pricing or performance of specific commercial products.
995|Heading2|3.4 General Architecture Patterns
996|ListParagraph|Model–View–Controller (MVC): separates request handling (controllers), data (models) and presentation (views). SmartCart uses ASP.NET Core MVC with Razor views.
997|ListParagraph|Repository pattern: isolates data-access code behind interfaces, allowing controllers to remain thin. SmartCart uses interface + repository pairs for each module.
998|ListParagraph|Layered architecture: presentation, application and data layers with clear dependencies.
999|ListParagraph|Database-centred business rules: rules implemented in stored procedures so that they are enforced consistently at the data layer.
1000|ListParagraph|Token-based authentication: a signed token identifies the user on each request without server-side session storage.
1001|Normal|SmartCart combines MVC, repositories and database-centred rules within a single deployable web application, which suits the size and budget of a single store.
1002|Normal|

1034|Heading2|3.6 ASP.NET Core
1035|Normal|ASP.NET Core is Microsoft’s cross-platform, open-source framework for building web applications. Its MVC model maps URLs to controller actions that return views or JSON. The request pipeline is composed of middleware components, which SmartCart uses for global exception handling, request logging and admin session enforcement. Built-in dependency injection is used to register interfaces and repositories, and the authentication framework supports JWT bearer tokens, which SmartCart reads from cookies.
1036|Normal|
1037|Heading2|3.7 SQL Database / Stored Procedure Approach
1038|Normal|A relational database is appropriate for SmartCart because its data is highly structured and related: categories contain sub-categories, products belong to sub-categories and brands, SKUs belong to products, stock rows belong to SKUs and purchase lines, and orders reference customers, products and SKUs. Referential integrity, unique constraints and transactions are required to keep these relationships consistent.
1039|Normal|Stored procedures encapsulate SQL logic inside the database. In SmartCart they are used for storefront operations (shop listing, product detail, cart, wishlist, customer authentication, checkout and orders) and for admin order processing. Placing rules such as “only active, non-cancelled, SellOnline items can be ordered” and “stock must cover the ordered quantity” in procedures ensures they are applied consistently. Procedures are created with CREATE OR ALTER so that patch scripts can be re-run safely.
1040|Normal|Other admin modules use Entity Framework Core mapped to the same tables. The schema itself is not generated by EF migrations; it is maintained by versioned build and patch SQL scripts.
1041|Heading2|3.8 HTML/CSS/JavaScript/Frontend Technologies
1042|ListParagraph|Razor views generate HTML on the server for both areas (shared layouts _Layout, _AdminLayout and _AdminAuthLayout).
1043|ListParagraph|Storefront styling uses custom style sheets (storefront, shop listing and bag styles).
1044|ListParagraph|Admin UI uses the Mantis Bootstrap template; SCSS is compiled to CSS during the .NET build using AspNetCore.SassCompiler and Bootstrap sources from npm.
1045|ListParagraph|JavaScript performs AJAX requests (fetch) to controller actions that return JSON, for example product lists and admin list pages.
1046|ListParagraph|Shared admin components include a toast notification system and an empty-state handler.
1047|Normal|Single-page application frameworks were not used; server-rendered pages with targeted AJAX calls were considered sufficient for the project’s scope.
1048|Normal|
1049|Heading2|3.9 Security Concepts
1050|Normal|Web applications handling customer data and business operations must address confidentiality, integrity and availability. Relevant concepts include: protecting credentials through one-way password hashing; preventing SQL injection through parameterised queries; validating all inputs on the server; restricting file uploads by type and size; avoiding disclosure of internal error details; and ensuring that users can access only the data and functions appropriate to their role.
1051|Heading2|3.10 Authentication and Authorization
1052|Normal|Authentication establishes who the user is. SmartCart uses JSON Web Tokens (JWT): after a successful login the server issues a signed token that is stored in an HTTP cookie and validated on subsequent requests. Customers and employees are separate identities stored in different tables and use different cookies and token audiences.
1053|Normal|Authorization determines what an authenticated user may do. SmartCart implements role-based access control for employees: each role is mapped to a set of permission codes (for example, Module.Action) grouped by module; each admin action declares the permission it requires; and the permission set is re-read from the database on the request rather than trusted from the client.

1592|Heading1|CHAPTER 6: SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE AND DESIGN
1593|Heading2|6.1 Design Overview
1594|Normal|The design of SmartCart translates the requirements and analysis into an application structure. The system is designed as one ASP.NET Core 8 MVC web application containing both the storefront and the admin panel, with a single relational database. This design suits a single store: it is simple to deploy and maintain, and it keeps customer and administrative data consistent. This chapter describes Current Architecture / SmartCart Now; future architecture is addressed only in Chapter 12.
1595|Heading2|6.2 Design Principles
1596|ListParagraph|Separation of concerns: controllers handle requests, repositories handle data access, views handle presentation.
1597|ListParagraph|Thin controllers: controllers validate input, call a repository and return a view or JSON.
1598|ListParagraph|Programming to interfaces: each repository implements an interface registered for dependency injection.
1599|ListParagraph|Rules close to the data: stock, online-sale and order-status rules are enforced in stored procedures.
1600|ListParagraph|Separate identities: customers and employees have different tables, cookies and token audiences.
1601|ListParagraph|Least privilege: every admin action requires an explicit permission.
1602|ListParagraph|Soft delete: master records are cancelled (Cancelled flag) rather than physically deleted.
1603|ListParagraph|Re-runnable scripts: schema changes are delivered by patch scripts that can be applied repeatedly.
1604|Normal|

1769|Normal|SmartCart’s current design is a layered, single-application architecture in which the storefront and admin panel share one database. 1793|Heading2|7.1 Database Overview
1794|Normal|The SmartCart database stores all catalogue, inventory, customer, order and security data for the store. It is organised into functional table groups: identity and access control, catalogue and variant attributes, media, purchasing and stock, cart and wishlist, orders with payments and shipping, and homepage content. A small number of tables exist in the scripts for future or legacy use but are not used by the current application; these are identified separately.
1795|Normal|The schema is documented in a DBML file that can be imported into dbdiagram.io to regenerate the ER diagram, and it is created by SQL scripts rather than by Entity Framework migrations.
1796|Heading2|7.2 Database Technology
1797|Normal|SmartCart uses a relational database management system. During development, Microsoft SQL Server (LocalDB/Express) is used, accessed through Microsoft.Data.SqlClient, Dapper and EF Core’s SQL Server provider. The connection string key in configuration is “Ecommerse”. Stored procedures implement the storefront and order logic. No user-defined SQL functions or views are used.

1803|Heading2|7.3 Database Design Principles
1804|ListParagraph|Naming: newer tables use ID_{Entity} primary keys and FK_{Entity} foreign keys (e.g. ID_Product, FK_SubCategory); older storefront/order tables keep legacy keys (UserId, OrderId, CartId, PaymentId, ShippingId).
1805|ListParagraph|Soft delete: most tables carry Cancelled, CancelledOn and CancelledReason; queries exclude cancelled rows.
1806|ListParagraph|Online visibility: SellOnline (default 0) on Products and ProductVariants; storefront shows only rows that are active, not cancelled and SellOnline = 1.
1807|ListParagraph|SKU-level stock: quantities are stored as rows in Stock keyed by FK_ProductVariant; available quantity is the sum of non-cancelled rows.
1808|ListParagraph|Snapshots: OrderItems store product name, SKU, variant label and prices at the time of ordering.
1809|ListParagraph|Uniqueness: unique product slug, SKU, barcode, customer e-mail, admin user name, permission code and module name.
1810|Heading2|7.4 Normalization
1811|Normal|The schema is normalised to third normal form for master data. Categories, sub-categories, brands, products and SKUs are held in separate tables, so each fact is stored once. Variant axes (e.g. Size) and their values are stored independently and linked to SKUs through ProductVariantAttributes, which removes repeating groups. The role–permission relationship is resolved through the UserRolePermissions associative table.
1812|Normal|Controlled denormalisation is used where history must be preserved: order lines copy product name, SKU and prices, and the order stores the delivery address fields, so that later catalogue or address changes do not alter past orders.

3022|Heading2|10.5 Authentication Implementation
3023|Normal|Customer registration hashes the password with the ASP.NET Core Identity password hasher and calls RegisterUser. Customer login retrieves the account with GetUserByEmail, verifies the hash and generates a token with UserJwtTokenService, which is written to the customer cookie. Admin login is handled by POST /api/admin/auth/login through AdminAuthRepository and AdminJwtTokenService. The JwtBearer OnMessageReceived event reads the token from the appropriate cookie; token validation accepts the configured issuer and both audiences.
3024|Normal|For admin requests, AdminAuthMiddleware rejects requests without a valid employee session (except login and access-denied), and RequirePermission loads the current employee’s permissions through IAdminAuthInterface.GetCurrentAsync before allowing the action.

3031|Heading2|10.6 API / Business Logic Implementation
3032|Normal|Business logic is divided between the application and the database. 3405|Heading1|CHAPTER 12: SECURITY, LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE SCOPE
3406|Heading2|12.1 Authentication
3407|Normal|Customers and employees authenticate separately against the Users and AdminUsers tables. Passwords are hashed with the ASP.NET Core Identity password hasher and are never stored in plain text.
3408|Heading2|12.2 Authorization
3409|Normal|Employee authorisation is role-based: roles map to permission codes grouped by module, and admin actions declare [RequirePermission("Module.Action")]. Permissions are re-read from the database on the request, so client-side button hiding is not relied upon.
3410|Heading2|12.3 Input Validation
3411|Normal|Input models use DataAnnotations; stored procedures validate business inputs such as address, phone and pincode formats, quantities and status transitions. Uploads are limited to jpg/jpeg/png/webp images up to 2 MB and one mp4/webm product video up to 20 MB.
3412|Heading2|12.4 SQL Injection Protection
3413|Normal|Database access uses parameterised stored-procedure calls through Dapper and parameterised queries generated by EF Core; user input is not concatenated into SQL statements.
3414|Heading2|12.5 Session/Token Security
3415|Normal|JWTs are signed and validated for issuer and audience, and are stored in separate cookies for customers and employees. Database trace logging masks password and token parameters.
3416|Heading2|12.6 Access Control
3417|Normal|AdminAuthMiddleware protects all /admin and /api/admin paths; customer-only features require a logged-in customer; customers can access only their own cart, wishlist and orders. GlobalExceptionMiddleware prevents unhandled errors from exposing internal details [verify production behaviour].
